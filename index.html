How Many Tubes Can We Plug?

TUBES 230 · OD 20 / ID 18 mm · 5 m
PASSES 1 shell / 2 tube
DUTY 1,268.7 kW

Corrosion has thinned the tubes. Plugging a leaking tube is quick and cheap, and it keeps the shell and tube sides apart — but every plug you drive takes area away. Plug too many and the exchanger can no longer make its duty, and the answer becomes a re-tube. Read the NDT report, then call it: how many tubes can you plug and still run?

3  How the rating works

Two effects, pulling opposite ways
  • 01Plugging removes tubes, so the available area falls: A_a = ntubes × π do L.
  • 02The same milk flow now passes through fewer tubes, so the velocity rises — and with it Re, Nu and hi. A plugged exchanger is a better heat-transfer surface per square metre than a full one.
  • 03A higher hi raises Ud, which lowers the required area: A_r = Q / (Ud F ΔTlm). So both curves fall. The question is which falls faster.
  • 04Nusselt depends on the flow regime, and plugging can move you between them: Re < 2100 laminar, 2100–10,000 transition, > 10,000 turbulent. Pick the wrong correlation and hi is meaningless.
  • 05The exchanger is operable while A_a ≥ A_r. F is computed from P and R rather than assumed, and comes out at 0.8669 for these temperatures. Duty, inlet temperatures and ho are fixed; fouling is neglected.

Milk flow is throughput, so it sets the duty: the milk must still reach 70 °C, and the hot water — still 5 kg/s at 140 °C — simply leaves colder. Push the flow up and Q rises while ΔTlm and F both fall, so the required area climbs from three directions at once and the plugging limit collapses. At 6.5 kg/s, only 7% above design, this exchanger cannot make its duty with nothing plugged. Past about 8.9 kg/s a 1 shell / 2 tube arrangement runs out of correction factor altogether — F collapses towards zero and no amount of area helps. That is the arrangement failing, not a temperature cross: the water is still leaving near 52 °C, well above the milk inlet. The fix there is more shell passes, or two exchangers in series.

ho is the strongest lever here, precisely because the shell carries about three quarters of the resistance — doubling it takes the limit from 24 tubes to over a hundred. Plugging raises hi, the side that was never controlling, which is why it buys so little.

Built on Case Study 2 of Heat Transfer — Application: Shell & Tube Heat Exchanger 4 (Kritchart Wongwailikhit, Ph.D., Heat and Mass Transfer System), slides 22–32. The app follows the lecture’s conventions for area and resistance — available area on the tube OD, 1/Ud = 1/ho + 1/hi without area correction — but computes the LMTD correction factor from P and R rather than reading it off a chart, giving F = 0.8669 where the lecture rounds to 0.86. On the lecture’s own Ud that change alone takes the required area from 66.31 to 65.78 m²; the app reaches 65.70 because it also evaluates the transition correlation at Re2/3 — the exponent the lecture’s own Nu = 40.74 corresponds to — rather than the Re0.67 printed beside it. Together they move the plugging limit from 21 to 24. Conventions matter here: computing the available area on the tube ID instead, or area-correcting Ud, would move the limit much further.
